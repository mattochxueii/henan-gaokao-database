## A

Saturday at Fenwick Mill

The old mill is opening its doors to people who would like to see how grain becomes flour. All three activities below are included in the entrance ticket. On arrival, show your ticket at the yard gate and collect a paper plan. There is no mobile signal inside the mill.

Meet the Millers | 9.45 and 13.45 | 35 minutes

Follow a working miller from the sacks of grain on the ground floor to the great stones above. The tour involves two steep flights of stairs. Book a place online before Friday evening. Arrive ten minutes early, as the group enters the working area together.

Flour Lab | 10.30 to 12.30 | about 25 minutes

At a table in the yard, compare three flours and try turning a small stone mill by hand. Groups start whenever a table becomes free. Put your name on the list at the table on Saturday morning. If the yard is wet, the activity moves to the cart shed beside the gate.

Mill on Film | 11.00, 12.00 and 14.00 | 20 minutes

Our film follows one sack of grain through the upper rooms. The ground-floor cinema has level access from the yard. Seats cannot be reserved. Doors open five minutes before each showing.

Bringing a picnic? The orchard beyond the yard is open until 15.00. Please keep your entrance ticket if you go out there, as you will need it to come back through the gate.

## B

My first paid job in a theatre was to paint a kitchen wall. On the stage floor lay a wooden frame covered with cloth. Vera, the designer, wanted the cloth to look like the brick wall of an old bakery. By lunch I had finished a square of perhaps thirty bricks. Each had its own tiny cracks. I had even painted a thin green line where the wall would have met the ground.

Vera pulled two chairs to the back of the hall. From there I could just make out a dark patch the size of a suitcase. She handed me a brush nearly as wide as my hand. I thought of my little cracks and said, rather sharply, "I suppose you'd like it done by tea?"

"It would help," she said. "But that's not why."

We went back to the cloth. She dipped the big brush in brown paint and dragged it almost dry across three rows at once. I could have done that blindfolded. Then she asked me to take the two chairs back down the aisle while she switched on the stage lights. From the same seats I saw a long mark that might have been left by a shelf, and above it the pale patches where jars had stood for years. The little cracks were still there. I knew because I had painted them.

On opening night a girl sitting beside me whispered to her father, "They've taken the bread down." There was no bread in the script. For the rest of the scene I found myself wondering who had moved it and why. When the lights came up, my father, on my other side, asked which part I had painted. I could have pointed out my square. Instead I pointed to the pale strip running halfway across the kitchen. "We had a shelf there once," I told him.

## C

At a sailing club, new members learn to tie a particular knot by watching a short film. The instructor's hands fill the screen. One end of the rope goes behind the other, disappears for a second and comes out through a loop. Beginners often stop the film just before the end disappears. The club's teaching group wondered whether the pause button was doing more than simply making the lesson longer.

The group invited beginners to work in pairs in separate rooms. Which member went first was decided by drawing a name. The first learner could pause the film whenever they wished, but could not go back. The second saw a recording of exactly what had appeared on the first learner's screen, including every pause. Both had a rope and could practise throughout. After the film, almost everyone could copy the knot with the red end on the left, as it had been on screen.

Then the instructor handed each learner a rope with the red end on the right. More of those who had controlled the film tied the knot correctly this time. An observer noticed that one of these learners turned the rope in her hands before she started. "I was looking for the bit that was about to disappear," she explained. Her partner had spent the same long pause trying to pull his loose end straight.

The two beginners had watched identical pictures for the same number of seconds. Yet the frozen picture had arrived in response to a question for one of them and in the middle of a quite different difficulty for the other. There is another possibility, however. Just knowing that she could stop the film might have encouraged the first learner to predict the next move more often, even when she let the film run. The club kept the button. It also began asking learners watching together to tell their partners what they wanted to see before anyone pressed it.

## D

Every evening at six, a bakery near my flat sells a paper bag of whatever is left on its shelves. The contents are a surprise. One Tuesday I carried home five brown rolls, a lemon cake and something I eventually identified as an onion pie. A card by the till said the shop had halved its bread waste in a month. I had seen the nearly empty shelves, so I believed it. Then, on Friday, I found two of those rolls at the bottom of my bag.

What worried me was a small journey taken by the word "waste". At the shop it meant bread the baker had to throw away. By the time I read the card I had understood it to mean bread that nobody ate. It was perfectly possible for the first figure to fall faster than the second. Of my five rolls, three had become lunches, but the other two had simply changed bins.

Asking customers how much they throw away would help, although the answers could be as misleading as the empty shelves. Imagine leaving a pile of cards by the door for willing customers to fill in. A man who has turned yesterday's loaf into a fine pudding may be rather more eager to tell his story than someone who left a bag unopened on the bus. Fifty cheerful replies would still leave us wondering about the people who walked straight past.

I would keep selling the bags. Mine cost less than buying the things separately, and I enjoyed finding a use for the pie. I would change the notice, though. Say how much less bread the shop itself throws away, then invite customers chosen by chance to keep a brief record of what happens at home. Even a modest set of such records, with a note of how many people declined, would tell a reader where the good news ends and the unanswered question begins.

On the morning we were moving out of our flat, my little sister May went missing. I found her in the kitchen with her back against the pantry door. A pair of bare feet stuck out from under her yellow raincoat, although it was a perfectly dry day.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Being four," she said. Then she stood on tiptoe. "Now I'm nearly six."

I knew what she meant. For every birthday Dad had stood us against that white door and drawn a line above our heads. Beside each line he had written a name and a date. There was a shaky red one where May had tried to measure me herself. The highest blue line was mine, made only last week. With the coats and pictures gone, that door looked more like us than anything else in the flat.

In the sitting room, Dad was folding the last empty box. Our new flat was only three streets away, but he had been carrying boxes up and down stairs since breakfast. When May asked him to take the door, he put the box down. "The next family will need somewhere to keep their food too," he said. "I'll give you five minutes before we lock up."

I took a picture of the marks with my phone. May looked at the screen, set the phone against the wall and tried to stand beside her red line. In the picture the whole door was shorter than her hand. She handed it back without a word.

Outside, the van driver sounded his horn. Dad went down to tell him we were nearly ready. Looking around the bare room, I noticed a roll of brown packing paper behind the bin. The white tape we'd used to close the boxes was still round my wrist.
